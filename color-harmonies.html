Color Harmony Generator — Free Online Tool

Color harmonies are combinations of colors that are visually balanced and pleasing based on their mathematical relationships on the color wheel. Rather than picking colors by trial and error, harmonies give you a systematic way to build palettes that work. Every major design system — from Material Design to Apple's Human Interface Guidelines — relies on color harmony principles to create consistent, professional-looking interfaces. Our tool calculates five harmony types from any starting color using HSL hue rotation, giving you instant palette suggestions for any project.

Complementary Colors

Complementary colors sit directly opposite each other on the color wheel, separated by 180° in hue. Blue and orange, red and cyan, yellow and purple are classic complementary pairs. This relationship creates maximum visual contrast and energy, making complementary schemes ideal for call-to-action buttons, alert badges, and any element that needs to stand out. The tension between complements draws the eye, which is why sports teams, flags, and fast-food brands use complementary schemes so frequently. Use them sparingly in UI design — a complementary accent against a neutral background is more effective than two equally weighted complements competing for attention.

Analogous Colors

Analogous colors are neighbors on the color wheel, typically within 30° of each other in hue. Blue, blue-violet, and violet form an analogous group. These palettes feel natural, cohesive, and calming because the colors share underlying pigment relationships. Analogous schemes are excellent for backgrounds, gradients, cards, and ambient UI surfaces where you want visual variety without visual conflict. Nature is full of analogous color — sunsets (red, orange, yellow), forests (yellow-green, green, blue-green), oceans (teal, blue, navy). In design, pick one dominant color, one supporting color, and one accent within the analogous range.

Triadic Colors

A triadic scheme uses three colors equally spaced on the wheel, 120° apart. Red, yellow, and blue — the traditional primary colors — form the most familiar triad. Triadic palettes offer strong visual contrast while maintaining balance, making them popular for vibrant, playful designs like children's products, creative portfolios, and event branding. The key to using triadic colors effectively is to let one color dominate (60%), use the second as a supporting accent (30%), and reserve the third for highlights (10%). This 60-30-10 rule prevents triadic schemes from feeling chaotic or overwhelming.

Split-Complementary Colors

Split-complementary is a variation of the complementary scheme. Instead of using the direct opposite, you take the two colors adjacent to the complement — offsets of +150° and +210° from your base hue. This gives you the contrast benefits of a complementary scheme with less visual tension, making it easier to balance. For a gold base (#C49A2A), the split-complements are a blue-violet and a blue-green. This scheme is often recommended for beginners because it is hard to get wrong — it always looks good and provides enough contrast for clear visual hierarchy without the harshness of direct complements.

Tetradic (Rectangle) Colors

Tetradic schemes use four colors arranged in two complementary pairs, spaced 90° apart on the wheel: 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270° from your base hue. This creates the richest, most diverse palette but also the hardest to balance. With four distinct hues, you have abundant variety for complex designs like dashboards, data visualizations, and editorial layouts. The key is restraint: pick one dominant color, use two as secondary accents, and reserve the fourth for small highlights. Avoid giving all four colors equal visual weight — the result will feel random rather than intentional. Tetradic schemes work best when the saturation and lightness are carefully controlled.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is a complementary color?

A complementary color is the color directly opposite on the color wheel, 180° away in hue. For example, blue's complement is orange, red's complement is cyan, and yellow's complement is purple. Complementary pairs create maximum contrast and visual energy, making them ideal for elements that need to stand out, like buttons and alerts.

What are analogous colors?

Analogous colors are adjacent on the color wheel, typically within 30° of each other in hue. Examples include blue, blue-green, and green, or red, red-orange, and orange. They create harmonious, low-contrast palettes that feel natural and cohesive — perfect for backgrounds, gradients, and ambient design elements.

How do I create a color scheme?

Start with your primary brand or base color. Use a harmony generator to find complementary, analogous, or triadic colors. Choose the harmony type based on your design goals: analogous for calm and cohesive, complementary for energetic and bold, triadic for vibrant and balanced. Then adjust saturation and lightness to create tints and shades for your full working palette.

What is a triadic color scheme?

A triadic color scheme uses three colors equally spaced on the color wheel, 120° apart. The classic example is red, yellow, and blue. Triadic schemes offer strong visual contrast while maintaining color balance. Use the 60-30-10 rule: 60% dominant color, 30% secondary, 10% accent to keep the palette from feeling chaotic.
